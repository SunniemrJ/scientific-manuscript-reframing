# Discussion Reasoning

## Purpose

Turn Results into scientific interpretation without repeating the Results section or overstating causality.

## Core paragraph logic

Use as many elements as the evidence justifies:

> Result → Meaning → Explanation → Literature comparison → Implication → Qualification

Not every paragraph needs all six elements.

## 1. Start from the study finding

Open with the finding or interpretation that the paragraph will explain.
Avoid opening with a broad literature review unless that comparison is the point of the paragraph.

## 2. Explain what the finding means

Ask:
- What scientific idea does this result support, challenge, refine, or complicate?
- Is the important point the direction, magnitude, heterogeneity, interaction, inconsistency, or limitation?
- Does the result resolve the gap introduced earlier?

## 3. Propose explanations at the correct strength

Separate:
- directly tested mechanism;
- interpretation supported by several results;
- explanation consistent with prior literature;
- plausible hypothesis for future testing.

Use language that reflects the level of evidence.

## 4. Use literature to compare, not decorate

A citation should do one of four jobs:
- show agreement;
- show disagreement;
- provide a possible explanation;
- establish broader significance.

Avoid strings of citations that do not advance the argument.

## 5. Build synthesis paragraphs

When several findings interact, use a synthesis paragraph to answer a higher-level question.
Examples:
- Why do different analyses converge or diverge?
- Why can a stable average conceal strong heterogeneity?
- Why does a factor explain some outcomes but not others?
- Why do two apparently similar groups behave differently?

This is often where the manuscript's novelty becomes visible.

## 6. Translate to implication carefully

Implications may be:
- theoretical;
- methodological;
- practical;
- policy-related;
- design-related;
- clinical;
- operational.

Do not jump from a surrogate or model result directly to an intervention recommendation unless the evidence supports that step.

## 7. Discuss limitations as boundaries of inference

Prioritize limitations that change interpretation:
- sampling or selection bias;
- measurement error;
- model assumptions;
- omitted variables;
- limited external validity;
- uncertain mechanism;
- scale mismatch;
- multiple-comparison or multiplicity issues;
- observational design;
- lack of independent validation.

Avoid generic limitation lists that do not affect the main claim.

## Discussion-level checklist

Before finalizing a subsection, ask:
- Does it explain rather than repeat?
- Does each paragraph have one job?
- Does literature comparison change the interpretation?
- Are causal claims calibrated?
- Are alternative explanations acknowledged when necessary?
- Does the section end closer to the paper's central contribution than it began?
