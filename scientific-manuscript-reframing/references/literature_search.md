# Literature Search and Source Attribution

## Use at every stage

Search relevant research papers before giving scientific or editorial advice at each applicable workflow step, using the confirmed journal, journal category, or provisional readership and the current decision. Reuse already verified articles when relevant, and run focused searches for new questions as the workflow advances. No fixed paper count is required. Select enough relevant evidence to explain the recommendation, including conflicting findings where material.

Keep a compact source record in the conversation with the search question, date, queries or sources consulted, verified bibliographic details, original link, access level, relevant finding and stage it informs. Carry this record forward with confirmed decisions. Update the search when a major decision or target changes.

## What to search at each step

| Workflow step | Search purpose |
|---|---|
| Journal preference | Verify official scope and look for recent papers matching the topic and contribution. Use a few relevant categories to help the author choose. Verify any specific journal examples before recommending them. |
| Evidence inventory | Check related studies for definitions, measurement conventions and typical comparisons. Attribute the manuscript's measurements and results to the supplied materials. Use discrepancies to frame questions for the author. |
| Diagnosis | Compare the paper's question, contribution, validation and inference with relevant field research. Use the comparison to explain specific weaknesses and priorities. |
| Whole-paper central idea | Examine established and recent findings, alternative explanations and related contributions to calibrate novelty and generality. A limited search cannot establish an absolute first-ever claim. |
| Argument and section design | Inspect how comparable research connects questions, evidence and conclusions and how target readers encounter them. Explain why the proposed sequence suits this study's evidence. |
| Results and optional synthesis | Search comparable analytical approaches, reporting practices and validation examples. Treat any proposed additional analysis as a proposal requiring the author's agreement and actual data. |
| Methods alignment | Check primary method papers, validation studies, definitions and applicable standards for the actual analyses reported. Flag missing information for the author. |
| Discussion | Search agreement, conflicting results, candidate mechanisms, limits of inference and justified implications. Explain which parts are directly supported and which remain hypotheses for this study. |
| Introduction | Verify the proposed research gap and objectives against closely related research, considering recent developments and relevant foundational work. |
| Conclusion, Abstract and Title | Check novelty and scope against the relevant literature and inspect comparable summaries. Use sources in the accompanying explanation. Apply the journal's convention for citations in these manuscript components. |
| Journal adaptation and submission materials | Recheck official guidance and comparable articles to explain fit, emphasis and proposed cuts. Attribute scope claims to official guidance and comparisons to the papers inspected. |

For local copyedits within an already approved section, use the stage's verified literature where applicable. Do not invent a reference for a language preference. If a new scientific or structural recommendation arises, perform the corresponding focused search.

## Source verification

Prefer original research, primary method papers, original datasets and official journal guidance. Reviews can orient the search but trace central scientific claims to primary studies when available. Open the original article, publisher record or verified DOI landing page before citing. Check author names, year, title, venue and the relevant finding. Search snippets alone are insufficient.

State when only the abstract is accessible and restrict the inference accordingly. Label a preprint as such and check for a published version where feasible. If search or access is unavailable, state the limitation, provide only verifiable advice based on accessible material, and ask whether the author wants to supply papers or proceed provisionally. Keep unresolved literature-dependent claims open.

## References and original links in advice

Place the supporting reference next to each literature-based recommendation or its paragraph. At first use provide author or first author et al., year, article title, venue and a clickable original article or verified DOI link. A compact source list can provide full details for later author-year citations, provided each recommendation remains traceable to its supporting source. Use the platform's supported citation format in generated documents.

Explain what the cited paper found and how it supports or limits the recommendation. Label your extrapolation as an inference. Cite the supplied figure, table or section for a claim about the manuscript. A proposed organization or wording preference should be identified as editorial judgment, with any analogous papers cited as examples of practice.

External studies provide context, comparisons and hypotheses. They cannot supply missing results for the manuscript, establish causation in its observational data or validate its models without a relevant test. Cite actual evidence rather than attaching a plausible reference to an unsupported statement.

Literature used to justify a revision should be visible in the conversation even when the revised manuscript component normally omits citations. Suggest additions to the manuscript bibliography with verified details and ask the author to confirm them as part of that stage's revision.
