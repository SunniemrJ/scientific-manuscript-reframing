# Manuscript Diagnosis

## Purpose

Identify the difference between what a manuscript currently says and what its evidence can most convincingly support.

## Diagnostic questions

### 1. Scientific question
- What question does the manuscript explicitly ask?
- Is that question precise enough to be tested or answered?
- Does the evidence actually answer it?
- Is a stronger or clearer question latent in the existing analyses?

### 2. Evidence structure
Inventory:
- direct observations or measurements;
- estimated effects or associations;
- model outputs;
- comparisons;
- robustness or validation analyses;
- qualitative or contextual evidence.

Separate strong evidence from exploratory findings.

### 3. Existing storyline
Summarize the manuscript in 3–5 sentences exactly as it is currently written.
Then ask:
- Does each section advance the same central argument?
- Are analyses presented in the order they were performed rather than the order needed for understanding?
- Are important findings buried in tables, figures, appendices, or late sections?

### 4. Fragmentation
Look for:
- many disconnected results;
- repeated descriptions without synthesis;
- several objectives that never converge;
- a Discussion that treats each result independently;
- an Introduction that promises a different paper from the one actually delivered.

### 5. Missing relationships
Ask whether the manuscript contains two or more evidence blocks whose relationship has not been tested or discussed.
Generic examples:
- predictor patterns vs observed outcomes;
- subgroup differences vs overall effects;
- model results vs experimental results;
- short-term vs long-term measurements;
- quantitative results vs qualitative observations;
- performance metrics vs practical consequences.

Do not invent a relationship if the data do not support one.

### 6. Novelty level
Classify the strongest contribution as one or more of:
- new dataset or measurement;
- new empirical pattern;
- new relationship;
- new mechanism or explanation;
- new synthesis/framework;
- new validation or falsification;
- new application or decision insight;
- new methodological capability.

If the novelty is currently stated only as a method or software choice, ask what scientific insight the method enables.

### 7. Claim risks
Flag statements that:
- convert association into causation;
- treat model output as direct observation;
- infer mechanism without testing it;
- treat non-significance as equivalence;
- treat classification as an intrinsic natural category;
- overgeneralize beyond the study population, scale, or design;
- use `significant` without statistical support;
- claim practical effectiveness from surrogate outcomes only.

Use `claim_strength_rules.md`.

### 8. Missing evidence
Identify which claims would require:
- additional analysis;
- sensitivity/robustness testing;
- independent validation;
- external literature;
- a more explicit limitation instead of more analysis.

### 9. Reviewer risks
List the 3–5 most likely reviewer objections, prioritizing:
1. mismatch between question and evidence;
2. unsupported novelty;
3. overclaim;
4. weak validation;
5. missing comparison/control;
6. unclear practical or theoretical significance.

## Standard DIAGNOSE output

### A. What the manuscript currently argues
3–5 sentences.

### B. What the evidence most strongly supports
3–5 sentences.

### C. Strongest contribution
One sentence.

### D. Structural problems
Ranked list.

### E. Claim risks
Short list.

### F. Reframing opportunity
One proposed central argument plus a section-level outline.

### G. Missing work
Separate into:
- essential before submission;
- useful but optional;
- not necessary if appropriately qualified.
