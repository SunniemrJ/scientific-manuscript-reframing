# Introduction, Conclusion, and Abstract

## Introduction

### Purpose

Lead the reader from a broad problem to the exact unresolved question addressed by the study.

### Generic progression

#### Paragraph 1 — Why the problem matters
Establish significance briefly. Do not over-explain background that does not lead to the research question.

#### Paragraph 2 — What is known and what is missing
Summarize the relevant literature and identify the specific limitation, contradiction, or unresolved relationship.

#### Paragraph 3 — Conceptual bridge
Explain the concept, theory, comparison, or analytical idea that makes the unresolved question tractable.

#### Paragraph 4 — Why this study/system/design is informative
Explain why the chosen data, system, experiment, population, or comparison is suitable for addressing the gap.
Keep descriptive detail to the minimum needed for justification.

#### Final paragraph — Objective and questions
State why the study was conducted and present objectives/questions in a clear progression.
Prefer a sequence such as:
1. characterize or establish;
2. test or relate;
3. interpret, generalize, or translate.

Do not repeat a method tutorial here unless the method itself is central to the scientific gap.

## Conclusion

### Purpose

Answer the paper's main question at study level.

A useful structure is:
1. **Study-level answer** — what problem was addressed and what was learned overall.
2. **Major findings** — only the findings needed to support the central claim.
3. **Key relationship and limits** — what the study explains and where that explanation stops.
4. **Broader implication** — theoretical, practical, methodological, or policy significance.

Avoid:
- repeating every numerical result;
- adding new literature review;
- introducing new analyses;
- claiming certainty beyond the design.

## Abstract

### Purpose

Provide the shortest complete version of the scientific argument.

Recommended sequence:
1. problem and research gap;
2. study approach;
3. two to four strongest findings;
4. main conceptual or practical contribution;
5. broader implication.

### Compression rule

When the abstract is too long, remove in this order:
1. secondary numerical detail;
2. long lists of variables or groups;
3. methodological implementation detail;
4. secondary findings;
5. contextual detail not needed to understand the contribution.

Do not remove the gap or the main conclusion to preserve minor results.

## Cross-section consistency

The same scientific core should appear at different levels of compression:

- **Introduction:** asks the question.
- **Results:** provides the evidence.
- **Discussion:** explains the evidence.
- **Conclusion:** answers the question.
- **Abstract:** compresses the entire argument.

If these sections emphasize different stories, the manuscript is not yet stable.
