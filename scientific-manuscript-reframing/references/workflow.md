# End-to-End Workflow

## Purpose

Use this workflow when a manuscript requires more than local editing. Reconstruct the scientific argument from the evidence upward.

## Stage-specific literature searches

At the journal-preference step and every subsequent scientific or editorial step, apply literature_search.md before presenting advice. Focus searches on the current decision, cite the relevant original papers alongside recommendations, and retain the author's confirmation sequence. Use the target journal or readership to select comparable work from both the target outlet and the wider field.

## Decisions to discuss with the author

Use the checkpoints below for a full manuscript. For narrower modes, start at the relevant checkpoint and use any confirmed upstream decisions.

| Step | What to show | What the author confirms before proceeding |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Journal preference | A short menu of relevant journal categories, or verified candidate journals with official scope links if helpful | The target journal, journal type, or an explicit decision to proceed with a provisional readership |
| 1. Evidence inventory | Key evidence with figure, table or section locators, limits of inference, and discrepancies between sources | Which materials and comparisons count as evidence, how to handle conflicting values or definitions, and which gaps remain open |
| 2. Diagnosis | The stated question versus the question the evidence answers, the strongest contribution, and the highest-impact problems | The priority for revision and whether to narrow a claim, retain it as unresolved, or consider additional analysis |
| 3. Central claim | A one-sentence research question and take-home claim, with genuinely distinct framings if available | Which contribution leads, how strongly it can be stated, and which findings are secondary |
| 4. Argument and section design | The full-paper logic and a section map specifying the central message, role, evidence, and connection to the next section for every applicable section and substantive subsection | The full-paper argument progression and the central message of each section, including any section or subsection the author wants to modify before drafting |
| 5. Results | Proposed subsection questions, figure/table order, and treatment of major and secondary results, followed by the agreed revision | What the Results emphasize, what moves or is condensed, and whether the revised text accurately represents the evidence |
| 6. Synthesis | The added insight, evidence for the link, alternative readings and a proposed synthesis if warranted | Whether to include the synthesis and whether it should be presented as a result, interpretation, or hypothesis |
| 7. Discussion | Proposed interpretation, competing explanations, limits of inference, and the strongest justified implications, followed by the agreed revision | Which explanations and implications to keep, at what claim strength, and which additional study evidence or focused literature checks are needed |
| 8. Introduction | Proposed gap, opening rationale, and research questions mapped to the agreed evidence, followed by the agreed revision | Which gap and questions to foreground, and whether the Introduction promises what the paper actually delivers |
| 9. Conclusion, Abstract and Title | Review these in order, showing proposed wording and asking for approval for each applicable item before moving to the next | The conclusion's scope, then which findings and qualifiers the abstract retains, then the emphasis and wording of the title |
| 10. Journal adaptation | Target audience, journal fit and the proposed emphasis, length or content cuts before drafting the requested materials | Which journal-facing framing and omissions to use, then whether the revised materials are accepted. Apply the same principle when only one item such as a cover letter is requested |

For an evidence conflict, show the actual values and their locations and explain what each reading would imply. If the author cannot settle it yet, ask whether to proceed with a provisional qualification or defer the affected claim. For a disagreement about emphasis, identify which finding each framing foregrounds and how the outline or implications would change. For submission copy, ask which two or three results should carry the case for fit and which contextual details can be shortened. Verify the current official scope and inspect comparable papers as part of the journal and literature workflow, and preserve the confirmed scientific argument.

## Phase 1 — Evidence inventory

Before rewriting, identify:
- study question or problem;
- study units, samples, datasets, or experimental conditions;
- measurements, variables, comparisons, models, or analyses;
- key figures and tables;
- robust quantitative findings;
- uncertain or weak findings;
- descriptive versus inferential analyses;
- practical or theoretical outputs, if any.

Create a compact evidence map:

| Evidence block | What was measured/analyzed | Robust finding | What it supports | What it does not support |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Present the evidence inventory and explicitly ask the author to confirm its accuracy or specify corrections. Describe the scope of any unresolved item. Stop here. Begin the manuscript diagnosis only after the author confirms the inventory, including any agreed treatment of unresolved items. An upload or correction alone does not approve progression unless the author also clearly confirms it.

## Phase 2 — Diagnose the existing manuscript

Start after the evidence inventory has been confirmed. Diagnose against that agreed evidence scope.

Identify:
- the research question the manuscript claims to address;
- the question actually answered by the evidence;
- whether sections follow analysis chronology rather than scientific logic;
- repeated findings across sections;
- methods presented as novelty;
- conclusions stronger than the analyses;
- important relationships that exist in the evidence but are not articulated.

Use `manuscript_diagnosis.md`.

## Phase 3 — Define the take-home statement

Present the whole-paper central idea, the scientific question, and the take-home message as connected proposals. Explain their evidence and scope. Ask the author to confirm or revise this interpretation before designing the argument around it.

Write one sentence that a reader should remember after a week.

A strong take-home statement usually contains:
- the main phenomenon or finding;
- the key relationship, contrast, or explanation;
- the broader scientific or practical significance.

Do not force a universal sentence template. Test the sentence:
- Is it directly supported by the study?
- Is it more than a methods summary?
- Is it broader than one isolated result?
- Does it express the paper's most important scientific contribution?

## Phase 4 — Build the argument structure

Use the structure that best matches the study. Common forms include:
- phenomenon → contrast → explanation → implication;
- question → evidence → relationship → interpretation;
- structure → process → outcome;
- intervention → effect → mechanism → consequence;
- observation → model/test → synthesis → generalization;
- problem → evaluation → comparison → recommendation.

Do not impose one template on every manuscript.

Before drafting, present a section map covering every applicable section, including Methods and the summary sections. For each substantive section or subsection, show the central message or methodological purpose, its role in the whole-paper argument, its evidence base, its internal progression, and its connection to the next section.

Ask the author to confirm the whole-paper logic and each section's proposed message, or identify the entries to revise. The author may approve the complete map together. Resolve disputed entries before drafting the affected sections. A list of headings alone is insufficient for this confirmation.

## Phase 5 — Rebuild Results

Order subsections so that each answers one distinct question and prepares the next.

A generic sequence might include:
1. data/model quality or primary descriptive result;
2. main contrasts or patterns;
3. relationships among key variables or analyses;
4. robustness, heterogeneity, or secondary tests;
5. integrative analysis or synthesis.

Use only the parts that fit the study.

Each subsection should contain:
- one question;
- the minimum evidence needed to answer it;
- one clear takeaway;
- a transition to the next question when useful.

Use `results_restructuring.md`.

## Phase 6 — Create a synthesis layer only when justified

A synthesis layer is useful when several analyses remain difficult to interpret separately.

Possible synthesis devices include:
- conceptual framework;
- empirical grouping;
- comparison matrix;
- composite index;
- latent dimension;
- state/process diagram;
- decision framework;
- relationship map.

Before creating one, ask:
- Does it compress genuine information rather than re-label existing results?
- Is it reproducible?
- Does it answer a scientific question not already answered by simpler analyses?
- Is uncertainty acknowledged?

Do not invent a synthesis device merely to make the paper appear novel.

## Methods alignment before Discussion

After the Results and any agreed synthesis, check that Methods describes the analyses actually reported. Present missing methodological details and any proposed restructuring to the author. Confirm substantive wording or interpretation changes before applying them. Distinguish reporting an existing analysis from proposing additional analysis.

## Phase 7 — Reconstruct Discussion

For each major discussion unit, move through as many of the following as the evidence justifies:

> Result → Meaning → Explanation → Literature comparison → Implication → Limitation

Not every paragraph needs all six elements, but the reasoning should advance rather than repeat Results.

Use `discussion_reasoning.md`.

## Phase 8 — Reverse-engineer Introduction

After the argument is stable, ensure the Introduction contains:
1. why the problem matters;
2. what is already known;
3. the specific unresolved gap;
4. the conceptual or analytical bridge used to address it;
5. the study objective/questions in a clear progression.

Describe the study system only to the extent needed to justify why it is informative.

Delete method tutorials unless the method itself is central to the scientific gap.

Use `intro_conclusion_abstract.md`.

## Phase 9 — Rebuild Conclusion and Abstract

Conclusion:
- answer the study-level question;
- summarize only the most important findings;
- state the key relationship and its limits;
- end with the broader significance.

Abstract:
- problem/gap;
- approach;
- strongest findings only;
- main scientific contribution;
- implication.

## Phase 10 — Adapt to target journal

After the science is stable:
- adjust emphasis and terminology;
- change the balance between theory, application, method, or policy as appropriate;
- update title, abstract, highlights, and cover letter consistently.

Never alter the evidence to fit a journal.

## Revision order agreed with the author

First establish the scientific decisions in this order.

> Journal preference confirmation → Evidence inventory confirmation → Diagnosis and priorities confirmation → Whole-paper central idea confirmation → Argument and section messages confirmation

Then explain and agree on the applicable rewriting order.

> Results → Optional synthesis → Methods alignment → Discussion → Introduction → Conclusion → Abstract → Title → Requested submission materials

At each section, recall its agreed central message and show its proposed paragraph or subsection progression. Ask whether the author confirms the plan or wants changes before drafting. After drafting, present the revised section and ask whether it is accepted or needs further work before proceeding to the next section. A previously confirmed plan can be reused when it remains applicable. Use the author's explicit instruction if they choose another order or combine stages.
