# Storyline and Conceptual Design

## Objective

Convert a collection of analyses into a scientific argument that progresses from evidence to a defensible contribution.

## Step 1 — Write the remembered sentence

The remembered sentence should describe the paper's main scientific contribution, not the software, dataset, or workflow used.

Weak:
> Several analyses were performed using a new pipeline.

Stronger pattern:
> The study reveals a consistent relationship between X and Y, shows where that relationship breaks down, and explains why this matters for Z.

Use the logic, not the wording.

## Step 2 — Choose the argument form that fits the study

Possible structures include:

### Pattern → relationship → explanation
Use when the paper begins with a descriptive finding and then explains it.

### Contrast → test → interpretation
Use when the paper compares groups, conditions, methods, or systems.

### Structure → process → outcome
Use when the study links organization or configuration to a downstream process or consequence.

### Intervention → effect → mechanism
Use for experimental or applied studies.

### Observation → model → validation → synthesis
Use when several analytical approaches converge on one conclusion.

### Problem → evaluation → recommendation
Use for engineering, management, clinical, or decision-oriented studies.

Do not default to any one structure. Let the evidence determine the argument.

## Step 3 — Identify the conceptual bridge

The bridge is the relationship that turns a descriptive paper into an explanatory or integrative one.

Generic bridge questions:
- Does X explain or predict Y?
- Does the effect of X depend on condition Z?
- Are two apparently different outcomes manifestations of the same process?
- Do different methods converge on the same conclusion?
- Does a summary metric conceal meaningful heterogeneity?
- Which factor explains why units differ?

A bridge is valuable only if it can be tested or reasonably assessed using the available evidence.

## Step 4 — State both explanatory power and limits

Strong papers often become more credible when they explain:
1. what a relationship or framework captures;
2. what it does not capture;
3. what additional factors or uncertainty remain.

Avoid deterministic wording unless the design supports it.

## Step 5 — Use groupings or frameworks cautiously

Create a type/class/profile only when:
- multiple dimensions need to be summarized;
- the rule or algorithm is reproducible;
- the groups are interpretable;
- the grouping helps answer a scientific question.

Use neutral labels unless independent evidence justifies a stronger natural or mechanistic interpretation.

## Step 6 — Test whether the synthesis adds information

Ask whether the new framework adds insight beyond:
- one dominant variable;
- one summary metric;
- a simple pairwise comparison;
- the original table or figure.

If not, remove it.

## Step 7 — Scale the contribution appropriately

Ask what level of generalization the evidence supports:
- within the sampled data;
- across study units;
- across methods or conditions;
- to a broader mechanism;
- to a practical decision context.

Do not generalize beyond the study design.
